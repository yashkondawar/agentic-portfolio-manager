# Sector Playbook — EPC, construction & concessions

model), VA Tech Wabag (JM Financial, Apr-26), an infrastructure-budget sector note (JM Financial,

## The economic engine
An EPC contractor **converts a contracted promise into revenue by spending working capital.** It has no
inventory to price, no brand to charge for, and no capacity constraint in the manufacturing sense — its
constraint is cash and execution bandwidth:

`Revenue = opening order book × execution rate + new inflow executed within the period`
`Value created = margin earned − cost of the working capital tied up to earn it`

This produces the family's defining inversion, stated in the pack and worth repeating because it changes
the reading order: **the balance sheet governs, not the P&L.** Revenue under percentage-of-completion
accounting is partly a management estimate; retention money, mobilisation advances, unbilled revenue and
contingent liabilities are where the business actually lives or dies.

Three further features:

- **Margin is bid at award, not earned in execution.** Whatever margin the project will produce was
  determined by the tender price years earlier. So a margin forecast is really a statement about the
  *mix and vintage* of the order book. PSP Projects' "EBITDA margins in the guided range of 11-13%" is
  the kind of disclosure to anchor on — and to test against the order book's composition.
- **Order book quality beats order book size, and the three questions are always the same** (per the
  family pack): who is the counterparty and how fast do they pay, at what margin was it won, and how old
  is it. A large book of aged, low-margin, state-government work is worse than a small clean one.
- **Concessions (BOT/HAM/annuity) are a different business bolted on.** They are levered infrastructure
  assets with equity commitments, and they must be valued separately from the EPC business — never
  blended into one multiple.

## Analysis sequence
1. **Order book, decomposed four ways** — by segment (buildings / roads / water / industrial /
   urban infra), by client type (central govt / state govt / PSU / private), by expected execution year,
   and **by age**. Then compute book-to-bill. PSP's note benchmarks its Rs 49bn order book against peers
   at Rs 121bn and Rs 102.3bn — peer order-book context is standard and useful.
2. **Order inflow and the bid pipeline** — inflow guidance and history, the bid pipeline, L1 positions,
   and the strike rate. Then the *award environment*: NHAI/MoRTH award rhythm, state tenders, and the
   election-cycle effect on awarding.
3. **Execution rate against the company's own history.** This is the conversion assumption the entire
   forecast rests on; a forecast implying an execution rate above anything achieved is the sector's most
   common failure.
4. **Margin by vintage and by segment.** Which parts of the book were bid in a competitive trough, which
   carry price-escalation clauses, and which are fixed-price. Then test the guided margin band against
   that mix.
5. **The working-capital anatomy, line by line** — receivables (split billed and **unbilled**), retention
   money and its release schedule, mobilisation advances received, inventory/WIP, and payables. Compute
   net working capital as % of revenue and as days. **Unbilled revenue is the single most important line
   in an EPC balance sheet** and it is where optimistic POC accounting accumulates.
6. **Counterparty payment behaviour by client type**, with ageing. Government receivables are seasonal
   and cluster at fiscal year-end; state-government exposure is materially slower than central.
7. **Contingent liabilities and off-balance-sheet exposure** — performance and advance bank guarantees,
   corporate guarantees to SPVs, disputed claims and arbitration awards (both directions), and equity
   commitments to concessions. **As a % of net worth**, this is the sector's real leverage.
8. **The concession portfolio, separately** — each asset's type (BOT-toll / BOT-annuity / HAM), traffic
   or annuity receipts, remaining concession life, SPV debt, equity invested and equity still committed.
9. **Promoter pledging and group leverage** — chronic across this family and a standing check.
10. **Then value the EPC business and the concessions separately, and sum.**

## Signature KPIs
| KPI | Formula | Unit | How to read it | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Order book** | value of unexecuted confirmed orders | INR cr | **With the four-way decomposition** (segment, client, execution year, age). Peer context helps (PSP Rs 49bn vs peers at Rs 102-121bn). Exclude L1 positions and MoUs — those are pipeline, not book | Company disclosure |
| **Book-to-bill** | order book / trailing revenue | x | Visibility, typically 2-3.5x here. **A rising ratio can mean slow execution rather than strong demand** — always read alongside the execution rate | Computed |
| **Execution rate** | revenue / opening order book | % | The binding variable. Benchmark the forecast against the company's own 5-year record and against the book's age profile | Computed |
| **Working capital days** | (inventory + receivables incl. unbilled + retention − advances − payables) / revenue × 365 | days | **The real capital employed and the growth constraint.** Rising WC days alongside revenue growth means growth is being bought with cash — the sector's classic deterioration path | Computed |
| **Contingent liabilities as % of net worth** | (guarantees + disputed claims + SPV commitments) / net worth | % | The off-balance-sheet leverage. A company with an "asset-light" claim and guarantees at 100%+ of net worth is not asset-light — verify in the notes, never from the strategy slide | AR notes |

## Supporting KPIs
Order inflow by year with guidance vs actual; bid pipeline and L1 position value; strike rate; order book
by segment / client type / execution year / age; average project size and tenure; share of book with
price-escalation clauses; fixed-price share; EBITDA margin by segment and the guided band (PSP: 11-13%);
billed vs **unbilled** receivables; retention money and its release schedule; mobilisation advances
received; receivable days by client type with ageing; net working capital as % of revenue; gross and net
debt, plus SPV-level debt separately; interest cover; cost of debt; bank-guarantee and letter-of-credit
utilisation against sanctioned limits (**the real constraint on bidding capacity** — a contractor that
has exhausted its BG limits cannot bid, whatever its balance sheet says); equity committed and still to
be invested in concessions; concession-asset traffic/annuity receipts, remaining life and SPV debt;
arbitration claims receivable and payable; promoter pledge %; ROCE and ROCE excluding concession equity;
fixed-asset turnover; own equipment fleet vs subcontracted share; subcontracting cost as % of revenue;
employee cost per rupee of revenue; segment/geographic concentration.

## Valuation convention
**P/E on executed earnings for the EPC business, plus SOTP where BOT/HAM/annuity assets exist — and the
balance sheet governs the multiple, not the P&L.** PSP Projects is stated at **17x FY25E EPS** (Keynote,
BUY, TP Rs 988), which is the standard form for a pure-play contractor with no concession portfolio.

Two disciplines the sector requires:

1. **Convert the order book at a defensible historical execution rate before it reaches earnings.** The
   order book is not revenue — the same rule as `defence_manufacturing`, and for the same reason.
2. **Value concessions separately and at their own economics** — a HAM asset is an annuity receivable
   from NHAI discounted at an appropriate rate; a BOT-toll asset is a traffic forecast; neither is an EPC
   earnings stream. Sum the parts and publish the implied blended multiple as a sanity check, as
   `specialty_chemicals` does.

**The multiple must be conditioned on working capital.** Two contractors with identical order books,
margins and growth, one at 60 working-capital days and one at 150, are not worth the same multiple —
the second consumes its own earnings to grow. Make that explicit rather than leaving it in the notes.

*Traps:* (i) applying a multiple to order-book-implied revenue rather than to executed earnings;
(ii) blending concession assets into the EPC multiple; (iii) ignoring contingent liabilities and SPV
guarantees when assessing leverage; (iv) crediting arbitration claims receivable as if realised —
they take years and settle at a discount; (v) capitalising a margin band that reflects a favourable
order vintage; (vi) missing that BG limits, not equity, cap the bidding capacity and therefore growth;
(vii) treating an election-cycle award surge as structural demand.

## Divergence cases

*Same verified fact, two defensible readings, and the evidence that settles them. The rule
and the closed vocabulary of conditioning variables are in `docs/OPINION_VS_ANALYSIS.md`
§7. `prompts/33_thesis_synthesis.md` seeds `state/interpretation_ledger.json` from these;
`prompts/34_thesis_redteam.md` checks 16-18 audit the result. A reading that names no
conditioner is an unearned adjective (§2 F6).*

**1. It trades at 17x FY25E EPS.**
- *Standard for a pure-play contractor* (`peer_set_choice`) — PSP Projects is stated this
  way (Keynote, BUY, TP Rs 988).
- *The P&L is not the constraint* (`balance_sheet_risk`) — the balance sheet governs the
  multiple here. Receivable days, retention money and mobilisation advances decide whether
  reported PAT ever becomes cash, and a contractor that cannot convert is worth less at the
  same EPS.
- *Discriminator* (`historical_distribution`) — CFO-to-EBITDA conversion over five years,
  and the receivables ageing.

**2. One P/E is applied to the whole company.**
- *It is one contractor* (`peer_set_choice`).
- *Concessions are not EPC earnings* (`capital_intensity`) — a HAM asset is an annuity
  receivable from NHAI discounted at an appropriate rate; a BOT-toll asset is a traffic
  forecast. Neither belongs inside an EPC multiple.
- *Discriminator* (`disclosed_mechanism`) — the concession portfolio valued separately at
  its own economics, with the discount rate and traffic assumption stated.

## Forensic screens (sector-specific)
- **Unbilled revenue growing faster than revenue** — POC accounting recognising profit the client has
  not yet certified. The single most important screen in this sector.
- Receivable days and retention money rising while revenue growth is presented as the story.
- Cost-to-complete estimates revised repeatedly on the same project; loss-making contracts not provided
  for (onerous-contract provisions absent from a book with a rising fixed-price share).
- Order book including L1 positions, LoIs, MoUs or framework agreements; the definition changed between
  periods; the same order counted at LoI and at contract.
- Order book not aged — a slow-moving or stalled project kept in the book indefinitely rather than
  written out. Ask which orders have had no billing for four quarters.
- **Contingent liabilities and corporate guarantees to SPVs excluded from the leverage discussion**;
  equity commitments to concessions not disclosed as a future cash call.
- Mobilisation advances treated as operating cash generation; a falling advance balance masking
  deteriorating cash flow.
- Arbitration awards recognised as income before realisation, or claims capitalised into contract assets.
- Capitalisation of bid costs, pre-construction costs or interest during construction beyond the
  permitted period.
- Subcontracting to promoter-related entities; related-party equipment hire or material supply;
  land or property transactions with promoter entities.
- Promoter pledging rising, or group companies' leverage being serviced by the listed entity's cash.
- Depreciation policy on the equipment fleet changed, or equipment sold and leased back to flatter ROCE.
- Segment reporting that nets concession-asset losses into EPC profits.
- Joint-venture/consortium accounting: the company's share of a JV's obligations understated, or JV
  revenue proportionately consolidated in one period and equity-accounted in another.

## Dependencies to map
**Government capex, at the level that matters** — Union and state budget capital-outlay lines, NHAI/MoRTH
award targets and actual awarding, Jal Jeevan/AMRUT for water, railway and metro capex, and the
scheme pipelines feeding the company's specific segments · **the award rhythm around elections**, which
is a real and repeating cycle in Indian infrastructure · government payment behaviour and its
fiscal-year-end seasonality; state-government fiscal health for state-exposed contractors · the model
concession agreement's terms for HAM/BOT and any revision · interest rates (working capital is
debt-funded, so rates hit the P&L directly) and bank/NBFC appetite for construction credit ·
**bank-guarantee capacity in the system**, which has repeatedly constrained mid-cap contractors ·
key input prices (steel, cement, bitumen, aggregates, copper) and whether the contract has
price-escalation clauses indexed to them · labour availability and migration patterns ·
land acquisition and environmental/forest clearance timelines, which are the most common cause of
execution slippage · arbitration and dispute-resolution reform (the Vivad se Vishwas-type schemes) ·
competitive intensity in bidding, which sets future margins · monsoon (a real seasonal execution
constraint).

## Common archetypes here
`capex-to-cashflow` (order-book conversion and concession assets maturing — the dominant archetype, and
the one whose execution-rate arithmetic must be shown) · `balance-sheet-repair` (deleveraging,
concession-asset monetisation, InvIT transfers — very common and often the real thesis) ·
`regulatory-tailwind` (government capex cycles and scheme pipelines — legitimate, but tie it to *awarded*
orders, not to budget headlines) · `turnaround` (working-capital and execution repair) ·
`market-share-gainer` where a contractor takes share as weaker competitors lose BG capacity ·
`deep-value-sotp`, common where the market prices a contractor with concession assets on EPC earnings
alone · `cyclical-recovery` on the award cycle. Treat `re-rating` with the standard skepticism weight,
and treat `quality-compounder` claims sceptically: durable outperformance here requires a working-capital
and bidding-discipline advantage that must be visible in the balance sheet across a full cycle, not
asserted from a good order-inflow year.
